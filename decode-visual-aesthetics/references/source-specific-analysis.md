# Source-Specific Analysis

Select the relevant medium instead of forcing every source through live-action cinematography vocabulary.

## Live-action film and television

Prioritize:

- production design and wardrobe palette;
- motivated light sources and time-of-day logic;
- probable lens family and camera height;
- blocking, eyelines, scale, and spatial power;
- camera movement motivation and stabilization character;
- coverage strategy and editing rhythm;
- exposure, highlight behavior, grain, halation, and finishing.

Do not claim an exact lens, stock, LUT, light fixture, or camera body without evidence. Use ranges and visual consequences: “wide-normal perspective with modest edge expansion” is better than inventing “a 28 mm lens.”

## Animation

Analyze:

- shape language and silhouette hierarchy;
- line weight, edge quality, cleanup, and contour variation;
- flat, cel, painterly, volumetric, or hybrid shading;
- palette per character, environment, and emotional state;
- background detail density versus character detail density;
- layout perspective and deliberate scale distortion;
- pose-to-pose timing, holds, overshoot, smears, and secondary action;
- frame cadence, compositing layers, parallax, and camera simulation;
- how animation simplifies or exaggerates physics.

Do not translate animation into live-action terms alone. “Soft light” may actually be a painted value grouping with no simulated source.

## Painting and illustration

Analyze:

- medium behavior: oil, watercolor, ink, pastel, print, collage, or digital simulation;
- pigment or color-mixing relationships;
- edge hierarchy: lost, found, hard, soft, broken;
- brush scale, stroke direction, impasto, wash, paper grain, or print texture;
- value grouping and focal contrast;
- spatial system: linear, atmospheric, flattened, decorative, or intentionally impossible perspective;
- cropping, balance, rhythm, and visual pathways;
- symbolic rather than physically motivated color and light.

When converting a static artwork to video, label all motion, depth, and camera decisions as new interpretations. Preserve the artwork's organizing logic, not the literal placement of every element.

## Photography

Analyze:

- subject-camera distance and probable focal family;
- available, shaped, or constructed light;
- shutter character and motion rendering;
- depth of field and focus placement;
- posing, gesture, environment, and moment selection;
- color treatment, local contrast, grain, and print or digital finish;
- series consistency across multiple photographs.

Separate what happened in front of the camera from what likely happened in grading.

## Advertising and music video

Analyze:

- brand or performer hierarchy;
- product visibility and material highlights;
- visual hook timing;
- choreography between camera, subject, graphics, and sound;
- motif repetition and transition design;
- color continuity across fast changes;
- whether spectacle serves recognition, desire, rhythm, or narrative.

## Mixed-reference sets

Assign one job to each source:

- palette source;
- lighting source;
- composition source;
- texture source;
- blocking source;
- motion source;
- editing source.

Do not average conflicting references into a vague hybrid. State which source governs each layer and resolve contradictions before prompting.

## Cross-medium translation

Use this sequence:

1. identify the source mechanism;
2. identify what the target medium can express directly;
3. reinterpret unsupported features;
4. preserve perceptual function;
5. test with an original subject and setting.

Example:

> Painted lost edges around a figure cannot be copied as brushwork in photoreal video without changing medium. Translate the function into low local contrast, atmospheric diffusion, shallow focus, and partial shadow integration.
