# Visual DNA Framework

Use this framework to move from visual evidence to reusable rules.

## Evidence labels

Label important findings:

- **Observed** — directly visible in the supplied source.
- **Repeated** — observed across at least three independent shots or compositions.
- **Estimated** — technically plausible but not confirmed, such as focal length or lighting ratio.
- **Unknown** — cannot be resolved from the available source.

Use “visual DNA” for repeated or composition-defining rules. Use “local choice” for isolated features.

## Sampling guidance

- **One still:** analyze visible color, tone, light direction, composition, depth, texture, blocking, and emotional effect. Do not claim editing or camera-motion rules.
- **Three to ten stills:** group by scene, time, and framing. Identify what survives across changes.
- **Video:** sample at cuts, camera-direction changes, blocking changes, exposure shifts, palette shifts, and emotional beats. Select 8–16 representative frames for a short clip.
- **Long film or series:** sample opening, ordinary dialogue, transition, high-intensity, intimate, exterior, interior, and ending sequences. Avoid deriving the whole system from a famous scene alone.

## Twelve analysis axes

| Axis | Observe | Convert into an instruction |
|---|---|---|
| Color | dominant, support, accent, hue separation, saturation hierarchy | define palette roles and where accents may appear |
| Tone | black point, highlight roll-off, contrast, exposure bias | define shadow floor, highlight behavior, and contrast curve |
| Light | direction, size, hardness, color, motivation, ratio, falloff | specify source position, softness, temperature, and fill restraint |
| Composition | balance, symmetry, negative space, crop, horizon, geometry | specify placement, empty-to-subject ratio, and dominant lines |
| Optics | probable focal family, distortion, compression, camera height | specify perspective behavior without pretending exact metadata |
| Depth | foreground, middle ground, background, focus, haze, occlusion | define layer count and focus hierarchy |
| Texture | grain, edge softness, halation, brushwork, material response | define surface behavior and finishing treatment |
| Blocking | body orientation, gaze, scale, distance, power relationship | define subject positions and relational geometry |
| Camera motion | path, amplitude, speed, easing, stability, motivation | define one movement and its emotional purpose |
| Scene motion | subject pace, secondary motion, environmental motion | define motion hierarchy and physical response |
| Editing | average shot density, cut trigger, transition, hold duration | define rhythm and where to pause or accelerate |
| Function | attention, emotion, narrative information, symbolism | connect the rule to the viewer response it serves |

## Palette analysis

Describe relationships before exact values:

1. dominant field color;
2. subject separation color;
3. emotional accent;
4. neutral bias;
5. highlight and shadow hue;
6. saturation distribution.

Use approximate hex values only when useful. State that sampled values may be affected by display transforms, compression, scans, or grading.

## Tonal analysis

Check:

- whether blacks are crushed, neutral, tinted, or lifted;
- whether highlights clip, bloom, or roll off gradually;
- whether the subject is separated by luminance, color, focus, or motion;
- whether contrast is global or localized;
- whether faces, fur, paper, paint, and reflective materials respond differently.

## Structural-rule test

For each proposed rule, ask:

1. Does it recur?
2. Does it organize attention?
3. Does it support the same emotional function?
4. Can it survive a different subject and setting?
5. Can it be stated as a production instruction?

If fewer than three answers are yes, keep it as an observation rather than visual DNA.

## Causal explanation format

Write each strong finding as:

> Evidence: what is visible.
>
> Mechanism: the spatial, optical, tonal, or temporal relationship.
>
> Effect: what the viewer notices or feels.
>
> Instruction: how to reproduce the mechanism with original content.
>
> Confidence: observed, repeated, estimated, or unknown.

## Fixed and variable grammar

Define:

- **fixed:** the few rules necessary for recognition;
- **variable:** scene-dependent choices that may change without identity loss;
- **conditional:** rules used only for specific emotions or story beats;
- **forbidden:** combinations that collapse hierarchy or create stylistic drift.

Prefer five to eight fixed rules. A style bible with twenty mandatory rules is usually too rigid to produce varied work.
